Case study · Premier League football club

Fewer queries, calmer fans, no extra seasonal staff

Merging three siloed teams into one let the club handle its busiest moments without extra staff, and answer anxious fans first.

I worked with the club as a consultant, including a spell as interim Head of Customer Service.

The challenge

Retail, ticketing and supporter services each ran as a separate team, each with its own peaks: membership renewals, kit launches, Christmas. So each brought in temporary staff. Fans sent the same question several times and were bounced between teams. A worried email about this weekend’s match waited in the same queue as one about a game weeks away.

What I did

EAudited every point where fans and local residents contact the club, across systems, processes and people.
MMapped the whole matchday experience, from buying a ticket to the day after, to find where questions start.
DMerged the three teams into one, rebuilt the help centre, added automatic replies and advance notices, and put questions about the next match first.

What made it work

One team could absorb every team’s peak, so one season’s rush was covered by staff who were quiet that month. Advance notices and the help centre answered questions before fans needed to ask. And a simple rule put the next match first, because that’s when fans get anxious.

Case study · Global sports and entertainment merchandise company

50+ online stores, one view of every order

Support agents went from hunting through dozens of shops to seeing a customer’s latest order the moment they open the ticket.

The challenge

The company runs online shops for sports teams and entertainment brands, with more than 50 stores worldwide. A single brand could have 7, split by country and currency. The help desk’s standard connection only linked to one store. So before agents could answer “where is my order?” (the most common question), they had to work out which shop the customer had bought from.

What I did

EConfirmed the standard set-up couldn’t cover multiple stores. Patching it store by store would have meant hundreds of separate rules to maintain.
MMapped how a ticket finds the right order: search that brand’s stores by the customer’s email address and return the most recent order.
DWrote the brief, designed the order panel and managed the developer through build and launch, testing it with the agents who’d use it every day.

How AI would help today

This project was done without AI: the brief, the designs and the build were done the long way. Today, AI could turn the agents’ requests into a first brief, build a clickable prototype the same day, and check the finished build against the brief. People would still decide what agents see first, and who is allowed to cancel or refund.

Choosing a platform partner, with prototypes as proof

Turning an ambitious brief for a mobile network with no call centre into a scored vendor shortlist, and a contract review that caught the gaps before signing.

Still in development. Not launched.

The challenge

The company wanted a fully digital mobile network across several countries, run without call-centre agents. Choosing the platform behind it is a long, expensive commitment. And what a vendor shows in a sales demo doesn’t always make it into the contract.

What I did

ECo-wrote the request for proposals and ran it across 11 vendors, then scored the shortlist of 7 on a 60% technical, 40% commercial basis.

AI pulled more than 20 earlier working conversations into one master requirements list. It compared the vendor’s draft line by line against that list, and built the prototypes. I wrote the review comments myself. Technical calls went to the technical lead, and commercial calls to procurement.

Case study · Travel eSIM business

Launching a travel eSIM, with AI as the design team

Using AI for the research, the journey design and clickable prototypes behind a product launch, so the team could see, test and change each idea far sooner.

The challenge

A travel eSIM business needed its customer journeys worked out for launch: finding a plan for several countries, buying for the whole family, topping up, pausing a plan. Normally that means weeks of workshops, write-ups and design before anyone can click through an idea. And every decision depends on how travellers really buy.

What I did

EResearched how travellers buy: who buys, for whom, and on how many devices. Then ranked the scenarios to build for first, such as a parent buying for a family of four.
MDesigned 12 customer journeys, from multi-country search and checkout to auto-renewal, pausing a plan, sending eSIMs to family and buying by message.
DTurned meeting recordings into around 400 traceable requirements, and shared each journey as a design to review and a prototype to try.

How AI helped

AI turned meeting transcripts into requirements, helped sort the research into scenarios, and built the wireframes and clickable prototypes. That made it much quicker to try an idea, show it and change it. I decided what to test, and the team agreed every requirement and design before it went to the developers.

Case study · giffgaff

99% self-service, and members who sold it for us

Building giffgaff’s automated member messages, community and referral scheme, so members helped themselves and each other, and brought in half of all new members.

I worked in-house as CRM Manager from 2009, setting up the CRM, member service and community.

The challenge

giffgaff launched as the UK’s first 100% online mobile network, with no shops and no call centre. Every member had to be able to get started, top up and sort out problems on their own. And mobile networks weren’t trusted: their satisfaction scores (NPS) were around 20.

What I did

MMapped the whole member journey, from welcome to repurchase, into 11 automated message flows (built in Neolane, now Adobe Campaign).
ESet up the community, with a positive culture where members answered members. This was before Google reviews and TripAdvisor were part of everyday life.
DRemoved friction from the start, with simple instructions in the SIM pack and clear messages about what to do next. Then I launched Recommended plan and Extra SIM.

What made it work

Being proactive, even when it cost revenue. I pushed hard for Recommended plan, based on what I’d seen work before. My case to finance: “I’d rather have 80% of something than 100% of nothing.” Members trusted a network that tried to save them money, and they told their friends.

The 11 member flows

Two flows, in action

Keeping members

Recommended plan

giffgaffBased on last month, a cheaper plan would cover how you use your phone. Want to switch? It only takes a minute.

Illustrative message, not the original wording

Members were told when a different plan would better suit how they used their phone, even if it meant they’d pay less. In focus groups it came up unprompted, and members posted screenshots on Twitter, along the lines of “giffgaff is still trying to save me money”. When asked why giffgaff, they said: because they try to save me money.

Members bringing members

Extra SIM

giffgaffYour SIM’s working. We’ve popped a spare in the post. Give it to a friend, and when they join, you both get £5.

Illustrative message, not the original wording

When a member activated their SIM, we sent them a second one to pass on to a friend. Both got something: £5 for the member and £5 for their friend. It put a ready-made invitation in every member’s hand.

The result

99%self-service, through the community and clear messages
70+ NPSwhen mobile networks averaged around 20, alongside the likes of Facebook and Google
50%of new members came through member referral
4 million+members, with the same basic flows still in use (as of 2022)
